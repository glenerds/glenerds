How to document deposit deductions

Every deduction needs three things: what it was for, what it cost, and proof it was necessary. Add each deduction above with a plain description — “carpet cleaning, unit 4B” beats “cleaning” — and the calculator keeps a running refund total. Print the itemized statement and send it with the refund: tenants who see organized paperwork rarely dispute it, and the ones who do will find you already prepared.

What you can and cannot deduct

Unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, and cleaning to return the unit to its move-in condition are the standard categories. “Normal wear and tear” is where most fights start: faded paint after three years is wear; a hole in the wall is damage. Photos at move-in and move-out settle more disputes than any calculator — take them every time, timestamped.

Worked example

$1,500 deposit, three deductions: carpet cleaning $180, wall repair $220, missing keys $75. Total deductions $475, refund $1,025. Add one more $1,100 repair and the tool flags that deductions exceed the deposit by $75 — the kind of edge case that’s easy to get wrong by hand.

Frequently asked questions

How fast must I return the deposit?

It depends on your state — commonly 14 to 60 days. The deadline is strict in most states, and missing it can cost you multiples of the deposit, so calendar it at move-out.

Can I deduct for normal wear and tear?

No. Wear and tear is the landlord’s cost of doing business. Only damage beyond ordinary use, unpaid amounts, and agreed cleaning qualify.

What if deductions exceed the deposit?

The calculator flags it. You can bill the former tenant for the balance, but collecting is a separate battle — small claims court is the usual venue.

Should I keep receipts?

Yes — every deduction should trace to an invoice or receipt. “Estimated” deductions without documentation are the first thing challenged.
