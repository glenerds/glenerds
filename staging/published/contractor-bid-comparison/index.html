How to compare contractor bids

Add each line item both contractors bid — demolition, electrical, plumbing, drywall, and so on — with each contractor’s price. BidCompare lines them up side by side, computes the percentage gap per item, and flags anything diverging more than 15%. An item priced by only one contractor is flagged too: that is either missing scope or scope the other contractor buried elsewhere. Totals at the bottom keep the big picture in view.

What the gaps are telling you

A big gap on one line rarely means one contractor is dishonest — it usually means they are not bidding the same thing. Different material grades, different assumptions about existing conditions, or one contractor missing the item entirely. The flagged lines are your negotiation script: ask each contractor to explain that specific number. Bids that survive those questions are bids you can trust.

Worked example

Kitchen demo: Contractor A bids $1,200, Contractor B bids $1,800. The gap is 33%, so the worksheet flags it. One line — “permit fees” — appears only in Bid A and is marked only one bid, so you know to ask Bidder B why it’s missing instead of discovering it mid-project.

Frequently asked questions

Why only two bids?

The free worksheet covers the classic head-to-head. Most investors collect three bids, which is where the paid tool takes over.

What if the bids use completely different line items?

Normalize them: break both bids into the same item list (the phases of the job), splitting lump sums where you have to. Comparison is only meaningful on matching scope.

Should I just take the lowest total?

Not without reading the gaps. The lowest total with three “only one bid” flags is often the most expensive bid once the missing scope gets added back as change orders.

What is a reasonable gap between bids?

Ten to fifteen percent on a line item is normal market variance. Past 20%, something structural differs — materials, assumptions, or missing work.
