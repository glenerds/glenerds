How to inspect a used espresso machine

Always see the machine run a real shot — powering on proves almost nothing. Work the checklist top to bottom: boiler behavior first (leaks and heat-up tell you the most), then water flow through each group, steam quality, the pump under load, and finally electrics and cosmetics. Mark anything that is not right as a flag or fail; the tally above converts your findings into a rough repair-cost range you can take into negotiation.

What the price should tell you

A used commercial machine at 30–40% of new is normal; below 20%, assume hidden work. Add your rough repair exposure to the asking price — that sum is the real price. If it lands near the cost of a new machine with a warranty, walk away. And never skip the serial number: it lets you (or a technician) check service history and whether the machine was reported stolen.

Frequently asked questions

Should I bring a technician to the inspection?

For machines over a few thousand dollars, yes — a paid pre-purchase inspection is cheap insurance. Use this checklist to screen machines first, so you only pay a technician for the ones worth a closer look.

What is the most expensive thing to go wrong?

Boiler and heat-exchanger work, followed by pumps and control boards. That is why the checklist starts at the boiler: a bad boiler can exceed the machine’s value.

Are the repair ranges accurate?

They are ballparks for budgeting and negotiation, varying by brand and region. Treat them as “order of magnitude” numbers, not quotes.

Does this cover home machines too?

The inspection logic applies to any pump-driven machine, but the checklist is tuned for commercial units — groups, steam boilers, and the price points where mistakes get expensive.
